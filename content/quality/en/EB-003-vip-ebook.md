# EB-003 VIP E-book: Quality Manager Playbook – the practice of quality management

## How to use this playbook
This e-book collects practical action patterns for a quality manager in a food factory: how to analyse a problem in the first minutes, handle a complaint and a customer conversation, prepare the site for a pre-contract audit, onboard a new customer, assess a machine, start up a new line and manage a technological change. Each chapter has steps, typical pitfalls and ready templates. Examples are generalised for learning and do not describe specific companies.

## 1. The quality manager's role
A quality manager does not "do quality" alone. They design a system in which everyone does quality and make sure the evidence exists. In practice they manage five areas:

| Area | Meaning | Pitfall |
|---|---|---|
| Risk | knows what could harm the product and the business and has controls | treating everything equally instead of prioritising |
| Compliance | law, standard, customer requirements | following changes "only before audits" |
| Product decisions | release, hold, withdrawal | yielding to sales pressure without data |
| People | training, culture, cooperation with production | being a "policeman" nobody talks to |
| Customer | complaints, audits, requirements | promises without checking site capability |

Three principles that protect a quality manager:
1. **Decisions are data-based and recorded.** Every product decision has a rationale that will survive an auditor's question a year later.
2. **Escalate early.** A problem reported to the director with an action plan is a problem to solve. A hidden problem is a crisis.
3. **Safety is not negotiable.** Commercial quality can be discussed with the customer; safety and legality cannot.

## 2. Analysing a quality problem

### 2.1 The first 15 minutes
| Minute | Action |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Understand the facts: what, where, when, how much, who noticed. See the problem yourself. |
| 5–10 | Contain: stop the process or hold product more widely than seems obvious. It is easier to release excess than to recall from the market. |
| 10–15 | Assess scale: has product left the site? Does it concern safety? Who must be told now (director, customer)? |

Do not start by looking for someone to blame. Start by securing the product and the facts.

### 2.2 Five-layer model
For every problem go through five layers – the cause rarely lies in only one:

| Layer | Questions |
|---|---|
| Product | What exactly is wrong? Which batch, how many units, is it repeatable? |
| Process | What were the parameters? Did anything change (raw material, settings, shift, staff)? |
| Equipment | Was there maintenance, a breakdown, repair, changeover? |
| People | Was the worker trained, did they have time, instructions, the right tools? |
| System | Why did our safeguards (checks, procedures) not detect the problem earlier? |

### 2.3 Analysis tools
| Tool | When to use |
|---|---|
| 5 Whys | simple problem with one main causal chain |
| Ishikawa diagram (6M) | many possible causes, teamwork |
| "Is / is not" analysis | problem affects only part of production: compare what differs between "good" and "bad" (line, shift, material, time) |
| Timeline | incidents with many events, finding the moment of change |
| Data analysis | logger, test and complaint data; trend charts |

### 2.4 When a cause is truly the root cause
A cause is a root cause when:
- after removing it the problem does not return,
- it lies in a system you can change (procedure, design, training, resources),
- you can show evidence (data, test, reproduction of the problem).

"Human error" and "operator carelessness" are symptoms. Keep asking: why did the system allow the error?

## 3. Customer complaints

### 3.1 A good opening
First response to the customer within 24 hours (or sooner for safety issues):
- acknowledgement and case number,
- what has already been done (e.g. stock of that batch held, analysis started),
- request for sample, photos, batch number,
- date of the next update.

Do not admit fault before analysis and do not deny without data.

### 3.2 Minimum data
| Data | Why |
|---|---|
| Product, batch, shelf-life date | traceability |
| Date and place of detection | whether the problem arose with us, in transport or at the customer |
| Description and photos | initial assessment |
| Sample | identification (e.g. type of foreign body) |
| Quantity affected | scale |
| Any consumer health effects | classification and possible crisis |

### 3.3 The 8D report
Many customers expect an 8D report. Structure:

| Step | Content |
|---|---|
| D1 Team | who analysed (quality, production, maintenance) |
| D2 Problem description | facts, figures, photos: what, where, when, how much |
| D3 Containment | hold, sorting, customer protection |
| D4 Root cause | cause of occurrence and cause of non-detection, with evidence |
| D5 Corrective actions | what we change to remove the causes |
| D6 Implementation and validation | dates, evidence of implementation |
| D7 Prevention | similar products, lines, procedures |
| D8 Closure | summary, team recognition, effectiveness review |

The key in D4 is separating two causes: **why the problem occurred** and **why our checks did not detect it**. Customers pay particular attention to the second.

### 3.4 Complaint pathway
| Stage | Owner | Deadline |
|---|---|---|
| Recording and acknowledgement | customer service | 1 day |
| Classification and containment | Quality Manager | same day |
| Analysis and sample testing | Quality Manager with team | by class (e.g. 5–10 days) |
| Report to customer | Quality Manager | per contract |
| CAPA and effectiveness review | action owner | 30–90 days |
| Trend analysis | Quality Manager | monthly |

## 4. Customer conversations
Rules for discussing a problem:
1. **Start with facts and actions**, not explanations.
2. **Say what you know, what you don't and when you will know.**
3. **Don't promise what you don't control** (e.g. "it will never happen again"). Promise specific actions and a deadline.
4. **Separate safety from quality.** On safety, speak clearly about risk and actions; on quality you can discuss tolerances.
5. **Confirm in writing** what was agreed by phone.

Example structure for difficult news:
| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Fact | "In batch 24-118 we found an incorrect date on 340 packs." |
| Impact | "The date is 2 days later than the shelf-life study supports." |
| Actions | "We have held stock in our warehouse. Please stop selling this batch." |
| Next step | "By 2 pm we will send the delivery list and a collection proposal." |
| Contact | "I am available on…" |

## 5. Customer and certification audits

### 5.1 Pre-contract preparation
Before signing with a new, demanding customer, check:
| Area | Question |
|---|---|
| Customer requirements | Do we have all documents (supplier code, specifications, label, detection and testing requirements)? |
| Certification | Does the customer require a specific standard and grade? |
| Technical capability | Can we meet requirements (e.g. detector sensitivity, "free from" claims, shelf life)? |
| Allergens | Does the customer's product bring a new allergen to site? |
| Testing | Which testing plan and laboratory are required? |
| Traceability | Can we meet the required test time? |
| Costs | Does the price include additional testing, audits, packaging? |

### 5.2 During the audit
| Stage | Advice |
|---|---|
| Opening | brief site presentation, show the flow and key changes since the last audit |
| Site tour | follow the product route; let the auditor talk to staff; don't answer for them |
| Documents | have ready: HACCP plan, audit results, CAPA, complaints, testing, training, traceability test, management review |
| Non-conformity | don't argue emotionally; ask for the requirement; discuss facts |
| Closing | make sure you understand each non-conformity; note deadlines |

### 5.3 After the audit
Prepare the action plan with root cause analysis for each non-conformity, not just a correction. Closure evidence (photos, records, procedures) must be unambiguous and dated.

## 6. Onboarding a new customer
| Step | Action | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Collect and review customer requirements | requirements list with compliance assessment |
| 2 | Gap analysis and action plan | plan with owners |
| 3 | Raw material and product specifications agreed with customer | signed specifications |
| 4 | Allergen and HACCP assessment for new products | updated analysis |
| 5 | Label artwork approved by both sides | dated approval |
| 6 | Factory trial and samples for approval | report, customer approval |
| 7 | Testing plan and laboratory per requirements | testing plan |
| 8 | Team training on customer requirements | attendance list |
| 9 | First deliveries under enhanced control | inspection records |
| 10 | Review after 3 months | report, lessons |

Common risks: customer requirements "buried" in contract appendices, differences between the customer specification and line capability, raw material changes without informing the customer.

## 7. Assessing machines and equipment
Before purchase and after delivery, assess equipment for hygiene and product safety.

### 7.1 Equipment assessment matrix
| Criterion | Question | Score (1–5) |
|---|---|---|
| Materials | Do contact parts have a declaration of compliance with Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004? Appropriate stainless steel grade? | |
| Hygienic design | No dead spaces, crevices or threads in the product zone? Smooth, self-draining surfaces? | |
| Cleanability | Can parts be removed without tools? Access to all areas? Resistant to chemicals and temperature used? | |
| Foreign bodies | Can parts loosen, crack or wear? Are there guards? | |
| Lubrication | Can lubricant reach product? Food-grade lubricants specified? | |
| Allergens | Can the equipment be effectively cleaned at allergen changeover? | |
| Measurement and control | Are critical parameters measured, recorded and protected from change? | |
| Service | Are parts, cleaning and maintenance instructions available in the local language? | |

Useful guidance: EHEDG (European Hygienic Engineering & Design Group) hygienic design guidelines and EN 1672-2 (food processing machinery – hygiene requirements).

### 7.2 Equipment acceptance
Before first production: technical acceptance, initial cleaning (removing manufacturing residues), cleaning validation, addition to the maintenance plan, glass and plastics register and instrument list, operator training.

## 8. Starting up a new line
| Phase | Quality activities |
|---|---|
| Design | layout review (flows, zones), hygienic requirements for equipment, HACCP review of the design, notification to the authority if the change requires updating registration or approval |
| Construction and installation | supervision of works (foreign bodies, glass, dust), protection of existing production |
| Acceptance | post-construction clean, hygienic acceptance, cleaning validation, instrument calibration, detector test |
| Trials | trials without product, with test product, factory trial; CCP validation |
| Start-up | HACCP plan, instructions, training, increased check frequency in the first weeks |
| Stabilisation | review after 4–8 weeks: results, complaints, environmental microbiology, faults |

Common problems: no time for validation "because the customer is waiting", no cleaning access (equipment too close to walls), metal detector without confirmed reject, no space for waste and tool storage.

## 9. Technological change
Every change (recipe, raw material, supplier, parameter, equipment, packaging) goes through change management. Quick test – does the change affect:

| Area | Question |
|---|---|
| Safety | Do hazards or CCP effectiveness change? |
| Allergens | Does the allergen list or cross-contact risk change? |
| Label | Do composition, nutrition, origin or claims change? |
| Shelf life | Could the change affect microbiological stability or quality? |
| Customer | Does the contract require notification or approval? |
| Law | Does the change require notification to the authority (e.g. new activity)? |
| Documents and training | Which documents must change and who must be trained? |

Rule: don't implement a change on Friday afternoon and don't implement several changes at once unless you must – if a problem occurs you won't know which change caused it.

## 10. Data and KPIs
Management needs information that enables decisions. A good monthly report fits on one page:

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Headlines | 3 sentences: what improved, what worsened, what you need |
| KPIs | complaints per million units, GMP audit results, CAPA on time, test results, quality costs |
| Trends | 12-month chart for 2–3 key KPIs |
| Risks | new risks (legislation, customer, supplier changes) |
| Decisions needed | specific requests: resources, investment, changes |

Costs of poor quality (write-offs, rework, complaints, discounts, extra testing, overtime for sorting) are the language management understands best. Calculate and show them.

## 11. Leadership and food safety culture
Food safety culture is what people do when nobody is watching. A quality manager influences it by:
- being present on the floor, especially on night and weekend shifts,
- recognising reports and line stops due to risk,
- explaining "why", not just "what",
- involving production leaders in GMP audits and problem analysis,
- consistency: the same rules for everyone, including management and visitors,
- measurement: survey, number of reports, observation results, staff turnover.

Example annual activities: an "allergen week" with short training at workstations, a quality board with results and complaints, a quarterly award for the best report, management meetings with staff at the line.

## 12. Case studies

### Case A – incorrect allergen label
**Situation:** a coating supplier changed the recipe and added milk powder. The specification reached purchasing but not quality. Product with the new coating was packed in labels without milk for 6 days.

**Actions:** stock hold, batches and customers identified, risk assessment (milk – allergen, risk of serious reaction), decision to recall with consumer information, authority and certification body notified, notice agreed with the authority, new labels.

**Root cause:** supplier specification changes not linked to change management and label review; no contractual requirement for suppliers to notify changes before implementation.

**Lessons:** every new specification version goes to quality; a raw material composition change triggers review of the allergen matrix and labels; key suppliers sign a commitment to give advance notice; quarterly confirmation that specifications are current.

### Case B – complaints about a "musty" odour
**Situation:** 6 complaints in a month about an unpleasant odour of biscuits, different batches, one market.

**"Is / is not" analysis:** the problem affects only one customer and one type of outer case; it does not affect other customers with the same product. Testing showed odour transfer from cartons stored in the customer's damp warehouse and a new carton batch with a different ink.

**Actions:** complaint to the carton supplier, odour test at receipt of new packaging batches, joint inspection of the customer's warehouse, odour requirements added to the packaging specification.

**Lesson:** not all causes lie in the production process – analyse the whole chain.

### Case C – new retail customer
**Situation:** a retailer wants to launch a private-label product in 8 weeks. It requires IFS Higher Level certification, metal detection with a specified sensitivity, a "palm oil free" claim and a 4-hour traceability test.

**Approach:** gap analysis in week 1 (detector sensitivity borderline for product in metallised film – packaging change or X-ray needed), realistic schedule agreed with the customer, supplier assessment for the claim, factory trial in week 5, traceability test in week 6.

**Lesson:** early and honest capability analysis protects against promises the site cannot keep.

### Case D – Listeria in a drain
**Situation:** environmental monitoring found Listeria spp. in a drain (zone 3) in the high-care area, twice in one month.

**Actions:** intensive cleaning and disinfection of the drain, vector swabbing within several metres (including zone 2), review of trolley and staff movement, check of product results (negative). Dismantling the drain revealed a damaged grate seal with residue.

**Lesson:** a repeated result at the same point is a niche; structural action is needed, not just stronger chemicals. Periodic drain dismantling and inspection was added to the maintenance plan.
