## 1. Why a business needs a quality system
A quality system is not a binder for the inspector. It is how everyday decisions are made: what we control, why, who is responsible, what evidence remains and what we do when something goes wrong.

A working system gives a business four things:
- **lower losses**: fewer complaints, returns, write-offs and downtime,
- **legal protection**: evidence that the business acted with due diligence,
- **market access**: retailers and larger customers require certification (e.g. BRCGS, IFS) or at least an effective HACCP system,
- **peace of mind**: you know what to do in a crisis.

The most common mistake at the start is buying a ready-made document set and leaving it on the shelf. Inspectors and auditors quickly see that the documents do not match reality. A document must describe what you actually do – and where practice is wrong, change the practice, not the description.

**Task:** list the last three quality problems on site (complaint, return, inspection remark). For each, note what it cost and whether you know why it happened.

## 2. Four pillars
| Pillar | Key question | Example hazard | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Safety | Can the product harm anyone? | Listeria in a salad, metal fragment, undeclared allergen | hazard analysis, CCP records, test results |
| Legality | Does the product meet the law of the market where it is sold? | allergen missing from label, unauthorised additive, short weight | approved label, specification, weight checks |
| Quality | Is the product what we promised the customer? | dry bread, wrong colour, different taste | specification, parameter checks, complaints |
| Authenticity | Is the claim true? | "100% honey" with added syrup, false origin | supplier assessment, origin documents, testing |

Safety always comes first: good taste or a low price cannot rescue an unsafe product.

## 3. What the law requires
Key EU legislation for every food business:

| Act | Subject | In practice |
|---|---|---|
| Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 | general principles of food law | you are responsible for product safety; you must know your supplier and customer (traceability); you must withdraw unsafe product and inform the authority |
| Regulation (EC) No 852/2004 | hygiene of foodstuffs | requirements for premises, equipment, water, staff, waste (Annex II) and HACCP-based procedures (Art. 5); since 2021 also food safety culture |
| Regulation (EC) No 853/2004 | food of animal origin | additional rules for meat, fish, milk, eggs; approval of establishments |
| Regulation (EC) No 2073/2005 | microbiological criteria | which bacteria are acceptable at which levels in each food category |
| Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 | food information to consumers | what must be on the label; allergens; allergen information also for non-prepacked food |
| National food law | national rules | registration or approval of the establishment by the competent authority, food handler health requirements, penalties |

Before starting operations, an establishment must be registered (and in some cases approved) by the competent authority.

Proportionality: the law allows a small business to have a simpler system than a large factory. The European Commission described this in Notice 2022/C 355/01 (implementation of food safety management systems, including flexibility for small businesses). Simpler, however, does not mean "none".

**Task:** check that your registration or approval decision is current and that its scope matches what you produce.

## 4. Prerequisite programmes – the foundation
Before analysing hazards, the site must meet basic hygiene conditions. These are prerequisite programmes (PRPs), also called good hygiene practice (GHP) and good manufacturing practice (GMP). Without them, no HACCP plan will work.

| Programme | Minimum that must work |
|---|---|
| Building and layout | sealed building, washable surfaces, flow from raw to finished without crossing "dirty" and "clean" |
| Water | potable water for production and cleaning, periodically tested |
| Cleaning and disinfection | cleaning schedule (what, with what, how often, who), correct chemicals and concentrations, effectiveness checks |
| Personal hygiene | health requirements, workwear, hand washing, illness reporting, no jewellery |
| Pests | pest control contract, bait station plan, reports |
| Waste | lidded bins, regular removal, licensed contractor |
| Suppliers | buying from checked suppliers, checking deliveries |
| Storage | correct temperatures, FIFO/FEFO, raw materials separated from finished goods and chemicals |
| Maintenance | working equipment, food-grade lubricants, glass and hard plastic control |
| Training | everyone knows what to do and why |
| Calibration | thermometers and scales checked |

The good news: in a small site many hazards can be controlled by well-run prerequisite programmes alone.

**Task:** walk the site with this table and mark each programme: working / partly working / not working.

## 5. HACCP step by step
HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) is a method, not a form. The Codex Alimentarius describes it in seven principles preceded by five preliminary steps.

### 5.1 Five preliminary steps
| Step | What to do | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Team | appoint people who know the product and process | in a small business this may be the owner, technologist and an experienced worker; what matters is knowledge of the site |
| 2. Product description | composition, parameters (e.g. pH, aw), packaging, storage, shelf life | one description may cover a group of similar products |
| 3. Intended use | who will eat it and how (as is or after cooking, children, allergic consumers) | products for children need a stricter approach |
| 4. Flow diagram | all steps from receipt to dispatch | do not forget rework, utilities and waste |
| 5. On-site confirmation | check the diagram on the floor | walk the process with the diagram in hand |

### 5.2 Seven principles
| Principle | Question | Bread example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Hazard analysis | What can go wrong at each step? | stones or metal in flour, bacteria surviving short baking, allergens (gluten, sesame) |
| 2. CCPs | Where is control essential and an error cannot be fixed later? | baking (temperature and time), metal detection after slicing (if present) |
| 3. Critical limits | Which value separates safe from unsafe? | baking parameters validated on site; rejection of a metal test piece of specified size |
| 4. Monitoring | How, how often and who checks? | oven parameter check every load, detector test at start and hourly |
| 5. Corrective actions | What do we do when a limit is breached? | stop, hold product since last good check, assess, repair |
| 6. Verification | How do we know the system works? | manager's record review, product testing, annual plan review |
| 7. Documentation | What evidence remains? | hazard analysis, HACCP plan, check sheets |

### 5.3 Hazard assessment in practice
For each hazard assess two things:
- **likelihood**: how often it could happen (1 – practically never, 5 – often),
- **severity**: how serious the consequences for the consumer are (1 – no health effect, 5 – life-threatening).

The product gives a risk score. High-scoring hazards need a specific control measure and a check whether the step is a CCP. Important: record the rationale. The inspector will ask "why did you rate this as low risk?".

### 5.4 Common mistakes
- Too many CCPs: everything marked critical, so nothing is really controlled.
- Limits without a basis: "we bake for 40 minutes because we always have" without confirming it is enough.
- Paper monitoring: records filled in at the end of the day "from memory".
- No action on deviation: an entry "too low" with no information about what happened to the product.
- HACCP plan not updated after changes to recipe, oven or supplier.

**Task:** draw a flow diagram for your main product and write one realistic hazard for each step.

## 6. Allergens in a nutshell
EU law lists 14 allergen groups that must be emphasised on the label (Annex II to Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011):

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| cereals containing gluten | crustaceans | eggs |
| fish | peanuts | soybeans |
| milk (including lactose) | nuts | celery |
| mustard | sesame | sulphur dioxide and sulphites (above 10 mg/kg or 10 mg/l) |
| lupin | molluscs | |

Five rules to start:
1. **Know what you have**: make a list of raw materials containing allergens (matrix), including allergens in additives, spices and improvers.
2. **Separate**: store allergens labelled, on lower shelves, in closed containers.
3. **Plan**: produce allergen-free products first, then those with the allergen.
4. **Clean effectively**: after allergen production – full clean and check.
5. **Label correctly**: the label must match the current recipe. Every change of raw material or supplier means checking the label.

"May contain…" is used only on the basis of a risk assessment where cross-contact cannot be eliminated. It is not a substitute for good cleaning.

## 7. Documentation that works on the shop floor
Rule: as many documents as needed, but none that nobody uses.

| Document | Purpose | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| HACCP plan | shows hazards and how they are controlled | manager, HACCP team |
| Procedures (e.g. cleaning, suppliers, complaints, withdrawal) | describe how a process is managed | managers |
| Work instructions | step-by-step how to perform a task | operators |
| Specifications | requirements for raw materials and products | purchasing, quality, production |
| Forms | space to record completion | operators |

A good work instruction:
- fits on one page,
- uses simple words and photos or pictograms,
- says what to do, how to check and what to do if the result is wrong,
- is available at the workstation in the current version,
- is in a language the worker understands.

Every document has a number, version, date and approver. When a new version is issued, old printouts disappear from the floor.

## 8. Records and evidence
Food safety follows the rule: **if it was not recorded, it was not done**. Records are evidence for you, your customer, the auditor and the inspector.

Good record-keeping:
| Rule | Good | Bad |
|---|---|---|
| At the time | entry at 10:15 after a measurement at 10:15 | filling in the whole day at 18:00 |
| Legible and permanent | pen, clear figures | pencil, correction fluid |
| Signed | initials of the person doing it | no signature, "team" |
| Truthful | actual result, even if bad | "nice" results always within limits |
| With reaction | out-of-limit result + what was done | out-of-limit result with no comment |
| Visible corrections | single strike-through, new value, date, initials | obliteration, torn pages |

How long to keep records? At least for the product shelf life plus a reasonable margin (often 12 months in practice), or longer if the customer or law requires.

"Always perfect" records raise suspicion. An honest record with one deviation and a described action shows the system works.

## 9. People and food safety culture
Since 2021 EU law (Regulation (EU) 2021/382 amending Regulation (EC) No 852/2004) requires businesses to build a food safety culture: employees understand why rules matter and feel safe reporting problems.

In practice:
- management shows commitment: is on the floor, asks, reacts, provides resources,
- an employee who stops a line because of a risk is praised, not punished,
- information flows both ways: results, complaints and changes are discussed with the team,
- training is practical and checked by observation, not just a signature on a list,
- culture is measured (e.g. a short annual survey, number of reports from staff).

Minimum induction training before starting work: personal hygiene and illness reporting, allergens on site, foreign bodies, workstation rules, who to tell about a problem.

## 10. When things go wrong
Every site has problems. The difference between a good and a bad system is what happens next.

### 10.1 Response scheme
| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1. Stop | stop the process or secure the product |
| 2. Label and isolate | "ON HOLD" label, separate location |
| 3. Assess | is the product safe, what about batches already dispatched |
| 4. Decide | release, rework, dispose – recorded decision |
| 5. Find the cause | ask "why?" five times |
| 6. Remove the cause | permanent action, not just a "fix" |
| 7. Check effectiveness | after a few weeks: has the problem returned? |

### 10.2 "5 Whys" example
Problem: cold store temperature 9°C in the morning.
1. Why? The compressor did not work properly overnight.
2. Why? The evaporator iced up.
3. Why? The cold store door was not fully closed.
4. Why? A damaged seal and no self-closer.
5. Why was it not noticed? No alarm and no door check at the end of the shift.

Actions: repair the seal, fit a self-closer, SMS alarm, "cold store door" on the shift close-down checklist, assess products that were in the store.

### 10.3 Product withdrawal
If unsafe product has left the site, the law (Article 19 of Regulation (EC) No 178/2002) requires immediate withdrawal and informing the competent authority. Prepare in advance:
- a list of customers with contact details,
- dispatch records with batch numbers,
- phone numbers of the competent authority,
- a test: pick a batch and check how long it takes to establish where it went and which raw materials it was made from.

## 11. Official inspections – how to prepare
Inspectors most often check:
- hygiene of rooms and equipment,
- personal hygiene and staff health requirements,
- storage temperatures and dates,
- HACCP documentation and records from recent weeks,
- labels and allergen information,
- traceability (where the raw material came from, where the product went),
- pest control and waste management.

Good practice during an inspection:
- one person accompanies the inspector and answers factually,
- documents are organised and available,
- problems are not hidden; it is better to show that a problem is known and there is an action plan,
- all remarks are noted; afterwards, defects are fixed on time and evidence kept.

## 12. 30-day implementation plan
| Days | Activity | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | site walk with the PRP table, list of gaps | prioritised action list |
| 4–7 | cleaning schedule, personal hygiene rules, health requirements check | basic PRPs in writing |
| 8–10 | list of suppliers and raw materials, allergen matrix | you know what you buy and what contains allergens |
| 11–15 | product descriptions, flow diagrams, on-site confirmation | HACCP foundation |
| 16–20 | hazard analysis, CCPs, limits, monitoring, corrective actions | HACCP plan |
| 21–23 | forms: temperatures, cleaning, CCPs, goods-in | records ready to use |
| 24–26 | training for the whole team, instructions at workstations | people know what to do |
| 27–28 | complaints and withdrawal procedure, contact list, traceability test | ready for problems |
| 29–30 | review of the whole system, corrections, signatures | system ready to use |

After 30 days, the most important things are consistent record-keeping and a short monthly review: what works, what does not, what problems occurred.

## 13. Glossary
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| aw (water activity) | amount of water available to microorganisms; the lower it is, the harder it is for bacteria to grow |
| CCP | critical control point: a step where control is essential for safety |
| FEFO | first expired, first out |
| GHP / GMP | good hygiene / manufacturing practice |
| Traceability | ability to establish where a raw material came from and where a product went |
| Cross-contact | unintended transfer of an allergen from one product to another |
| PRP | prerequisite programmes: basic hygiene conditions |
| RTE | ready-to-eat food requiring no further treatment |
| Validation | confirming that a control method is effective |
| Verification | confirming that the system works as planned |
